The Ghost Marketing Paradox: Why the People Driving Your Engagement Are Rarely the People Driving Your Growth

By Jasper Evangelista · Ghost Marketing: Navigating Dark Social and the Whisper Economy

About the book ↗

Every marketer has lived some version of these two stories.

In the first, a campaign takes off. The post is shared widely, the comments are glowing, the dashboard lights up, and the report to leadership writes itself. Then the quarter closes and sales barely move.

In the second, a quiet product with an unremarkable social presence keeps growing. Nobody can say why. The comment sections are empty, the follower count is modest, and yet customers keep arriving, and when you ask them how they found you, the answer is some version of "a friend sent it to me."

Both stories point to the same uncomfortable idea, and it is the foundation of Ghost Marketing: the people generating visible engagement are not necessarily the people generating growth.

I call this the Ghost Marketing Paradox. Too many marketing decisions still confuse the audience that reacts with the audience that buys.

The paradox, stated plainly

Public engagement metrics mix several different signals: impressions show exposure, clicks show an action, and likes or comments show visible response. All can be useful. None, on its own, proves purchase intent or commercial influence.

Meanwhile, some of the most consequential steps toward a purchase happen privately. Someone screenshots your pricing page and sends it to a business partner. Someone asks a group chat, "Has anyone used these guys?" Someone forwards your article to a colleague with a one-line note. Someone searches your brand name three days after a conversation you will never see.

The first group is visible and countable. The second, the ghosts, is harder to observe. And because teams tend to reward what dashboards show, budgets and creative attention can drift toward the loudest reactions instead of the decisions that build the business.

Why ghosts behave the way they do

It is tempting to read silence as indifference. Sometimes, silence accompanies serious consideration.

Public engagement carries a social cost. Depending on the platform and privacy settings, a like or comment can be seen by others. That visibility may turn a casual reaction into a statement about identity: this is who I am; this is what I endorse. People are selective about it, and many are selective to the point of never doing it at all.

Private sharing works differently. When you send something to one person or a small group, you are not broadcasting an identity. You are making a specific recommendation to a specific person, with context, and you are staking a small amount of your personal credibility on it. That is why a relevant forwarded link can carry more persuasive weight than a thousand likes. The sender is saying, I thought of you, and I think this is worth your time.

This is the Whisper Economy: the informal web of direct messages, group chats, private communities, email threads, and offline conversations where recommendations are actually exchanged and trusted. In a 2012 Atlantic essay, Alexis C. Madrigal popularized the term "dark social" for sharing that conventional referral analytics struggle to identify. The attribution gap persists today, even as private messaging has become an everyday part of digital life.

Dark social is a layer, not a channel

It's tempting to treat dark social as another line item in the media plan. That misses the point.

Think of dark social as an attribution blind spot that can cut across channels. A public post, newsletter, podcast, webinar or ad may spark a private conversation that your reports never connect back to the source. Public touchpoints can become raw material for private recommendations.

This changes the question you ask about any piece of marketing. The old question is, How many people engaged with this? The Ghost Marketing question is, What will people say about this when I am not in the room, and is it easy for them to say it?

How the dashboard misleads you

Three mechanisms keep the paradox hidden.

1. Attribution gaps. When a private recommendation leads to a purchase, analytics may classify the resulting visit as "direct" or attribute the conversion to a later measurable touchpoint, such as branded search or a retargeting ad. The conversation that helped shape the decision may be missing from the record.

2. Survivorship of the loud. Teams study the content that performed publicly and copy its traits: provocative hooks, simple takes, applause-friendly framing. Yet the content people forward privately may win for different reasons: usefulness, specificity and relevance to someone's immediate problem.

3. The measurement trap. Goodhart's law offers a useful warning: a measure can become less informative once people optimize primarily to improve it. Once likes and shares become the goal, teams get very good at producing likes and shares. Whether those behaviors correlate with growth quietly stops being checked.

The result is a feedback loop. We measure the visible, reward the visible, produce more of the visible, and gradually lose contact with the audience that actually decides.

Likes can signal identity. Forwards can deliver utility.

If you remember one distinction from this article, make it this one.

Sometimes people like content to signal who they are. They often forward content because it solves something for someone else. The first can reflect the sender's image. The second puts the recipient's need front and center.

That gives you a practical test, which I call the forward test. Before publishing anything, ask: Is there a specific person who would receive this from a friend and think, "thank you, I needed this"? If you can name that person and moment, you've given the content a plausible reason to travel through the Whisper Economy. If you cannot, rethink why anyone would pass it along.

Six principles for marketing to ghosts

These principles are a working framework, and they are deliberately practical.

1. Design for the forward. Build content around being passed along: a clear point, a self-contained format, and a reason it matters to a particular kind of recipient. Make it understandable without the surrounding context of your feed.

2. Hand people the sentence. Recommendations travel as short sentences, such as "they're the ones who do X without Y." If customers struggle to explain what makes you different, recommending you becomes harder. Give them a line worth repeating.

3. Be present at the moment of the question. Private conversations are full of recurring questions: Is it legit? How does it compare? What does it actually cost? Is it worth it? Create content that answers these plainly and keep it easy to find, so that when someone is asked, the answer is one link away.

4. Remove the public-act requirement. Do not make people comment, tag, or post to take the next step. Make your name easy to remember and search, make pages work when opened from a messaging app, and let interest convert without a public gesture.

5. Measure by triangulation. You should not try to monitor private conversations. But you can look for indirect evidence of their influence. Watch branded search trends, direct and unattributed traffic, sales-cycle changes, and free-text answers to "How did you hear about us?" Ask that question everywhere, and read the answers as a qualitative dataset rather than a tally.

6. Earn the whisper, never fake it. This is the ethical boundary of the whole approach. Ghost Marketing is about deserving a place in private conversations, not infiltrating them. Seeding fake recommendations, planting accounts in communities, or trying to surveil private channels is astroturfing, and it destroys the very trust that makes the Whisper Economy valuable. The durable strategy is simpler: be genuinely worth recommending.

What to do on Monday

You do not need to overhaul your strategy to begin.

  • Audit your attribution. Find where your analytics labels "direct" traffic and compare it with what customers say in their own words. The difference is a clue worth investigating, not a headcount of invisible customers.
  • Add the question. Put a free-text "How did you hear about us?" field on your checkout, signup, and contact forms, and have sales teams record the answer verbatim.
  • Run the forward test on your next five pieces of content, and rewrite the ones that fail.
  • Create one "private-ready" asset: a plain, shareable answer to the most common question prospects ask before buying.
  • Add ghost indicators to your reporting: branded search trend, direct traffic trend, and the top phrases customers use to describe you.

Three objections worth taking seriously

"If I can't measure it, how do I justify it?" You are already making decisions under imperfect measurement. Last-click attribution is not truth, it is a convention. Triangulated evidence from several imperfect signals is more honest than a precise number that measures the wrong thing.

"Isn't this just word of mouth?" In spirit, yes, and that lineage is the point. What has changed is that word of mouth now happens in writable, forwardable, screenshot-able digital form, which makes it faster, more scalable, and easier to design for, while becoming harder to see. Brands that dismiss it as a vague "nice to have" risk overlooking an important source of influence.

"Should I abandon public engagement?" No. Public content is how ghosts find you, how they vet you, and what they pass along. The shift is not from public to private but from treating public metrics as the goal to treating them as a means: a way of creating things worth whispering about.

The paradox, resolved

The paradox does not ask you to ignore the people who like, comment, and share. They matter. It asks you to stop mistaking them for the whole audience, and to stop assuming that the quiet majority is a disengaged one.

Some of your most valuable customers may never publicly acknowledge you. They may read, think, discuss and decide in conversations you cannot enter. Your task is not to drag them into the light but to make their private conversations about you easy, accurate, and favorable.

Stop waiting for the like. Start earning the whisper.
